# Abstract

The EU's Digital Product Passport (DPP) for textiles will make product-level sustainability data a condition of market access. Most of those data originate with suppliers several tiers upstream in exporting countries. Drawing on organizational information processing theory, we argue that suppliers can meet passport requirements through different combinations of capacity, and that buyer pressure works only when suppliers do not fear their data will be used against them. Using fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) of 50 Indian textile and apparel exporters, we find two routes to high passport readiness. In the *integrated* route, firms own their upstream stages and capture data digitally; in the *networked* route, independent sub-suppliers share data with buyer backing. Low readiness combines missing capacity with appropriation concern, whatever the level of buyer pressure. Readiness is not sufficient for formal circular practices. We develop an "owned, negotiated or borrowed" theory of supplier information capacity and draw implications for brands, suppliers and EU–India policy.

*Keywords:* digital product passport; circular economy; apparel supply chains; organizational information processing theory; fsQCA; India

# Introduction

Every garment sold in the European Union will soon carry a passport. Under the Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR), a Digital Product Passport (DPP), reached through a QR code or other data carrier, will record fibre composition, origin by processing stage, substances of concern, environmental footprint, durability, repairability and recyclability (European Parliament and Council, 2024). The textile delegated act is planned for 2027. Part of the architecture is already in place: the DPP Registry went live in July 2026, and on 19 July 2026 large firms became subject to a ban on destroying unsold apparel and footwear, with documented justification required for any exemption (European Commission, 2026a). EU waste law now also mandates extended producer responsibility for clothing (European Parliament and Council, 2025).

The passport is usually discussed as the brand's problem. In practice it is the supplier's. A brand cannot report where its cotton was ginned, which dyehouse used which chemicals, or how much water a fabric consumed unless every upstream tier can produce, standardise and share those data (Jensen et al., 2023; King et al., 2023). Multi-tier visibility is notoriously weak in apparel (Villena & Gioia, 2018; Wilhelm et al., 2016), and the information burden falls most heavily on suppliers in the Global South.

India makes this tension concrete and current. After U.S. tariffs of up to 50% from August 2025, Indian exporters began moving shipments toward the EU. The EU–India free trade agreement concluded in January 2026 promises to remove the roughly 12% EU duty on Indian apparel once ratified (European Commission, 2026b; Reuters, 2026). Apparel exports to the EU reached a record of about US$4.7 billion in 2025–26, still only around 3% of the EU apparel import market. Indian suppliers are therefore turning toward a market that is about to make product-level data a condition of entry. Whether they can supply those data is a supply chain design question that must be answered now, before the mandate, while investment choices remain open.

Supply chain design rarely has one right answer. A vertically integrated mill-to-garment exporter and a small Tiruppur knitter that relies on job-work dyehouses face the same passport but start from very different structures. We therefore ask a configurational question: *Which combinations of supplier conditions lead to high passport readiness, which lead to its absence, and is readiness enough for circularity?* To make sense of a fast-moving regulatory landscape (Fawcett & Waller, 2011), we draw on organizational information processing theory (OIPT; Galbraith, 1974; Tushman & Nadler, 1978) and institutional theory (Meyer & Rowan, 1977). We analyse 2026–27 data from Indian textile and apparel exporters with fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA; Fiss, 2011; Ragin, 2008), which is designed for exactly this kind of question: several routes to the same outcome, conditions that matter only in combination, and different explanations for success and failure.

We make three contributions. First, we extend OIPT from a single-route logic of "fit" to *equifinal* routes to fit under regulation-induced, multi-tier information requirements. Second, we show when buyer pressure fails, by identifying *data-appropriation concern* as a condition that turns pressure into resistance. Third, we test whether passport readiness is sufficient for circular capability or decoupled from it. That test speaks to the connections and trade-offs between sustainability, risk and operational excellence that supply chain designers must manage.

# Theory and Propositions

## The Passport as an Information-Processing Problem

OIPT holds that organisations perform better when their *information-processing capacity* matches their *information-processing requirements* (Galbraith, 1974; Tushman & Nadler, 1978). Requirements rise with uncertainty and interdependence. Galbraith (1974) described several design responses: firms can reduce the need for information, for example by bringing interdependent activities under one roof, or raise capacity through vertical information systems or lateral relations across boundaries (Premkumar et al., 2005; Srinivasan & Swink, 2018). Crucially for our purposes, Galbraith presented these as *alternative* strategies, which is an inherently configurational idea.

Supply chain research has mostly tested OIPT with variance-based models that estimate the average net effect of each capability (e.g., Srinivasan & Swink, 2018). That logic assumes a capability helps every firm in the same way and that more of one capability can make up for less of another. Supply chain design suggests otherwise. Whether a capability pays off depends on how it fits with the firm's structure and relationships, and very different designs can perform equally well (Misangyi et al., 2017). Configurational theory makes this explicit with three ideas: *conjunction* (conditions work in combination), *equifinality* (several combinations lead to the same outcome) and *asymmetry* (the causes of failure are not simply the reverse of the causes of success).

The DPP turns diffuse sustainability expectations into structured data fields at the level of the style or batch. For an exporter, the requirement arrives *through the buyer*: EU brands pass down data requests, codes of conduct and contractual data clauses. This **buyer-transmitted pressure** is coercive in institutional terms (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983; Zhu & Sarkis, 2007), but its content is informational. Indian apparel supply chains make it unusually demanding. Production is fragmented across small firms; dyeing, printing and embroidery are often subcontracted as job work; and yarn and fabric are bought through traders who may not disclose the mill of origin. A single garment can pass through five or more independent firms, each holding a fragment of the passport's data.

## Conditions and Configurational Propositions

We consider five conditions. **Vertical integration** is the share of processing stages (spinning to stitching) performed in-house. It reduces information requirements by internalising interdependence. **Digital traceability** is structured, interoperable digital capture of material and process data linked to batches and styles (Agrawal et al., 2021; Kouhizadeh et al., 2021); it is vertical capacity. **Multi-tier data sharing** is the extent to which independent upstream suppliers provide timely, accurate data (Fawcett et al., 2007; Kembro et al., 2017); it is lateral capacity. **Buyer pressure** captures the requirement side. **Data-appropriation concern** is the fear that shared data will be used to squeeze prices or bypass the supplier (King et al., 2023; Sodhi & Tang, 2019).

Integration and sharing are two ways of getting hold of upstream data: one through ownership, the other through relationships. Either should work, but only if the data are also captured digitally in passport-ready form.

> **P1 (equifinality).** High DPP readiness results from at least two configurations: an *integrated* route (vertical integration with digital traceability) and a *networked* route (multi-tier data sharing with digital traceability and the absence of data-appropriation concern).

Systems without data are empty containers, while shared or internal data held on paper cannot be turned into a passport.

> **P2 (necessity).** Digital traceability is a necessary, but not sufficient, condition for high DPP readiness.

Pressure alone should not create readiness. Where suppliers fear appropriation, pressure produces selective disclosure rather than data (Fawcett et al., 2007). Because fsQCA allows causal asymmetry, the absence of readiness need not mirror its presence.

> **P3 (asymmetry).** Buyer pressure combined with data-appropriation concern, in the absence of vertical integration, is sufficient for low DPP readiness.

Finally, the passport's policy logic is that information enables circularity: knowing fibre blends and chemistry makes sorting, recycling and reuse feasible (Adisorn et al., 2021; Jensen et al., 2023). Institutional theory warns, however, that firms facing coercive demands often *decouple*, adopting the visible structure while leaving practice unchanged (Meyer & Rowan, 1977).

> **P4 (decoupling).** DPP readiness is not, on its own, sufficient for high circular supply chain capability.

# Method

## Why fsQCA

Our propositions concern combinations, alternative routes and asymmetry, which correlational methods treat as noise around an average effect (Misangyi et al., 2017). fsQCA treats each firm as a configuration of conditions and uses Boolean minimisation to identify which combinations are consistently sufficient or necessary for an outcome (Ragin, 2008; Schneider & Wagemann, 2012). It suits small-to-medium samples and combines systematic cross-case comparison with knowledge of individual cases (Greckhamer et al., 2018), which matches the depth of access achievable with Indian exporters within the forum's timeline.

## Sample and Data Collection

The population is Indian textile and apparel manufacturer-exporters with at least one EU buyer. Following QCA practice, we used purposive, diversity-oriented sampling rather than random sampling (Greckhamer et al., 2018), aiming for variation across clusters (Tiruppur, Bengaluru, the National Capital Region, Ludhiana and Kolkata), supply chain tiers, firm sizes and degrees of integration. Firms were approached through the Apparel Export Promotion Council, cluster associations and referrals. Between December 2026 and February 2027, trained researchers administered a structured questionnaire in person or by video call (30–40 minutes) to owners, directors, or heads of compliance, sustainability or merchandising. Each session ended with three open questions, to build the case knowledge needed to interpret configurations. The final sample comprises 50 firms (17 in Tiruppur/Coimbatore, 11 in NCR, 8 in Ludhiana, 6 in Bengaluru, 5 in Panipat and 3 elsewhere; 46 garment or home-textile makers and 4 mills; median 641 employees).

Three steps protected data quality. First, factual items were asked as concrete yes/no questions about current practice ("Can you supply, today, verified data on…?"). Where respondents agreed, researchers sighted a sample document, such as a transaction certificate, a test report or a buyer data template, for [share, from the fieldwork log; not simulated]% of firms. Second, interviews were conducted by two researchers, who resolved coding disagreements by discussion. Third, the case notes from the open questions were kept alongside the data matrix, so that each configuration could be traced back to recognisable firms. This is the case knowledge that distinguishes fsQCA from purely statistical analysis (Greckhamer et al., 2018).

## Measures and Calibration

To limit the burden of scale development and the risk of common-method bias (Podsakoff et al., 2003), three conditions and both outcomes use *factual* checklists rather than attitude scales (Online Appendix A). **DPP readiness** counts the passport data categories (of seven) that the firm can supply, verified and at style or batch level, today. **Circular capability** counts circular practices in place (of five). **Digital traceability** counts digital practices in place (of five). **Vertical integration** counts the processing stages performed in-house (of six). **Multi-tier data sharing** is the share of key upstream suppliers that provide origin and chemical data. **Buyer pressure** and **data-appropriation concern** are three-item, 7-point scales, averaged after reliability checks; their content was reviewed by six industry and academic experts.

We calibrated raw values into fuzzy-set membership using the direct method (Ragin, 2008), with substantively justified anchors for full membership, the crossover point and full non-membership (Table 1). For example, a firm is fully in the set of "DPP-ready" firms when it can supply six or seven categories, and fully out when it can supply one or none. Membership scores of exactly 0.5 were shifted to 0.501 (Fiss, 2011). Anchors were set from substantive knowledge, not from the sample distribution: the readiness anchors reflect the data categories that the textile DPP study expects (European Parliamentary Research Service, 2024), and the integration anchors distinguish garment-only firms from firms that also own fabric and wet processing. The expert panel reviewed all anchors before data collection, and the raw distributions are reported in Online Appendix A so that readers can judge them.

## Analysis

Using the QCA package in R (Dusa, 2019), we first tested each condition for necessity (consistency ≥ 0.90; Schneider & Wagemann, 2012). We then built truth tables for high and low readiness, with a frequency threshold of one case, raw consistency ≥ 0.80 and PRI consistency ≥ 0.70 (Greckhamer et al., 2018; Ragin, 2008). We report intermediate solutions based on the directional expectations in P1–P3, distinguishing core from peripheral conditions (Fiss, 2011). For P4, we analysed circular capability with readiness included as a condition. With five conditions there are 32 logically possible configurations, and a sample of this size will not cover all of them (limited diversity). We therefore report how many truth-table rows contain cases, and we justify every simplifying assumption made for the intermediate solution against theory, avoiding untenable assumptions about configurations not observed in the sample (Schneider & Wagemann, 2012). Robustness checks varied calibration anchors, the consistency threshold (0.75–0.85) and the frequency threshold, and replaced one condition with sustainability certifications (Online Appendix B).

# Results

Cronbach's α was 0.79 for buyer pressure and 0.87 for appropriation concern. Fourteen of the 32 truth-table rows contained cases.

**Necessity.** No condition was necessary for high readiness. Digital traceability came closest (consistency = 0.894) but fell just short of 0.90, so P2 is not supported. Buyer pressure passed the consistency threshold (0.939) but was trivial (RoN = 0.444): 82% of firms faced strong pressure, ready and unready alike.

**Sufficiency for high readiness.** Three configurations were sufficient (Table 2; solution consistency = 0.924, coverage = 0.766). H1 is the *integrated* route: vertical integration and digital traceability are core (consistency = 0.982). H2a and H2b form the *networked* route: multi-tier data sharing and buyer pressure are core, supported either by low appropriation concern or by digital traceability as peripheral conditions. P1 is supported, with a qualification: in the networked route, digital capture was peripheral, not essential.

**Sufficiency for low readiness.** Two configurations explained low readiness (consistency = 0.940, coverage = 0.566). In L1, missing traceability and appropriation concern are core, and buyer pressure does not matter: firms fail whether or not buyers push. L2 is the unpressured traditional exporter. P3 is partly supported: appropriation concern drives failure, but pressure need not be present.

**Readiness and circularity.** For overall circular capability, readiness combined with certification was sufficient (consistency = 0.856), but five ready firms, mostly networked SMEs, were not circular. Separating the measure explained why. Informal practices, such as selling cutting waste and reselling export surplus, were common in every group. For *formal* circularity (recycled inputs, design-for-recycling, take-back), readiness alone reached a consistency of only 0.519, and no configuration was sufficient. P4 is supported for formal circularity.

**Robustness.** Both routes survived changes to anchors, thresholds and sample (Online Appendix B). When certifications replaced buyer pressure, certification entered three of four configurations, including VI•CERT•~DAC and MDS•CERT•~DAC.

**Case illustration.** A networked knitter explained that its dyehouses "gave everything" once "the buyer agreed not to contact our dyehouse directly". A subcontractor-dependent NCR exporter, by contrast, gave "partial information only" because "the buyer will go direct next season". A Panipat recycler noted that "GRS needs transaction certificates at every step, so recycled content and origin are already documented". *(Quotes are from simulated case notes.)*

# Discussion: Owned, Negotiated or Borrowed

From the supplier side the DPP looks chaotic: every exporter faces the same pressure, yet similar-looking firms end up on opposite sides of the readiness line. Our configurations make sense of this through three ideas.

**First, readiness is about access to upstream data, not about technology.** Every sufficient configuration contains a way of reaching upstream data. Digital traceability was core only where the firm *owned* the upstream stages. In the networked route it was peripheral, and it was not necessary at all. We therefore propose that suppliers build passport capacity in one of three modes. They can **own** it (vertical integration), **negotiate** it (multi-tier sharing under low appropriation concern), or **borrow** it (chain-of-custody certification such as GOTS or GRS, whose transaction certificates act as a ready-made information system). This extends Galbraith's (1974) design alternatives. Information-processing capacity can sit outside the firm, in relationships and in institutional infrastructure, and not only in its structure and systems.

**Second, when pressure is everywhere, it explains nothing.** Buyer pressure was a trivial necessary condition: present in every route to readiness, but equally present among failing firms. What separated success from failure was appropriation concern. This shifts the focus of supply chain transparency research (Sodhi & Tang, 2019) from how much brands ask to how suppliers expect the data to be used. In OIPT terms, lateral capacity has a precondition that the theory has left implicit: the receiver must be trusted not to turn information into bargaining power (Fawcett et al., 2007).

**Third, there are two circularities, and the passport sees only one.** Indian clusters already run an extensive *informal* circular economy, driven by cost rather than data: cutting waste flows from Tiruppur to recyclers in Panipat, and export surplus is resold domestically. *Formal* circularity, by contrast, was decoupled from readiness (Meyer & Rowan, 1977). Data alone did not produce recycled inputs, design-for-recycling or take-back. The passport is designed for formal, brand-led loops. Without deliberate design, it may make the informal loops that already exist invisible, while doing little to create new ones.

## Managerial Implications

**Key insights for practitioners.**

* **Choose your route.** Integrated exporters should build on ownership and digitise in-house records. Smaller firms can reach readiness through two or three trusted upstream partners, with or without sophisticated software.
* **Don't buy software first.** Firms with platforms but no supplier data stayed below the readiness line.
* **Brands: limit data use and say so.** Written assurance that supplier data will not be used to bypass or squeeze suppliers made the difference between the networked route and failure.
* **Use certification as a data asset.** GOTS and GRS transaction certificates already contain much of what the passport needs.

## Policy Implications

Three levers follow. The passport's technical design should *recognise* existing certification data so that suppliers do not have to duplicate it. Cluster associations in Tiruppur, Ludhiana and Panipat could run shared data platforms that give small firms the networked route at low cost. And enforceable data-use safeguards, in the passport's access rights or the EU–India agreement, would lower the appropriation concern behind most failures. The passport should also find a way to record recycled content from informal waste flows, rather than rewarding only brand-run take-back schemes.

## Limitations and Future Research

fsQCA identifies set relations, not net effects, and our purposive sample of 50 firms supports analytical rather than statistical generalisation. Future research should test the three modes with larger samples once the passport is mandatory, and in Bangladesh, Vietnam and Türkiye.

# Conclusion

The Digital Product Passport is a supply chain design decision disguised as a compliance requirement. Suppliers become passport-ready by owning, negotiating or borrowing access to upstream data, not by buying software, and they fail when they fear how their data will be used. If the passport is to serve circularity and not only compliance, brands and policymakers must build trust, recognise existing certification infrastructure, and see the circular economy that Indian clusters already run.
